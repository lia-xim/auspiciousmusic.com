MIDI Fighter Twister Review and Ableton Workflow

Sixteen endless encoders with RGB rings and push switches, in a box small enough to sit beside a laptop. Ten years on, what it does well, what it does not, and how it maps into a modern Live set.

What it actually is

Four rows of four endless encoders, each with a push switch and a ring of RGB LEDs. No screen, no transport section, no faders. It is a controller with exactly one idea: give sixteen parameters a physical knob, and let the ring show you where each one sits.

Because the encoders are endless rather than potentiometers, there is no value jump when you switch what a knob controls — the classic problem with a mapped potentiometer, where the first touch snaps the parameter to the knob's physical position. That single property is why the device has outlived controllers with far longer feature lists.

Banks are the whole point

Sixteen knobs is not many for a Live set. The device answers that with banks: four pages of sixteen, switchable from the unit itself, giving sixty-four assignments without touching the mouse.

This is also where most of the confusion starts. Banks live in the controller's own configuration, not in Live, so a bank switch sends a different set of CC numbers rather than telling Live to change page. Live simply sees more controls. Once that is clear, the mapping strategy follows: assign by bank, not by knob.

Mapping it into Live

  1. Open the vendor utility and confirm the encoders are in the relative mode Live expects. Sending absolute values here is the single most common cause of jumping parameters.
  2. In Live's preferences, enable the device as a control surface input and output. The output matters — without it the rings never light.
  3. Use MIDI map mode for the first bank and work left to right. Mapping out of order costs more time than it saves.
  4. Save the mapping into your template rather than the project. A controller layout that only exists in one set will be rebuilt from scratch a month later.

Relative mode is not one setting. There are several relative encodings in common use, and Live is particular about which it reads. If a knob moves a parameter in the right direction but at the wrong rate, the encoding is wrong rather than the mapping.

Where the ring lights help and mislead

The rings show a parameter's position, which is genuinely useful when four banks are in play. They are also the device's weakest point: feedback depends on Live sending values back, and a mapping made through certain routings never produces that return path. The ring then shows the last position the encoder was turned to rather than the parameter's real value.

It is a small thing until a bank switch, at which point every ring is confidently wrong.

What a decade shows

  • The configuration utility is a separate desktop application, which dates the workflow. Newer controllers configure from the host.
  • There is no transport, no clip launching and no display. That is a design choice, not an omission, but it means the device never works alone.
  • USB-B rather than USB-C on units of this generation, so an adapter is part of the kit on a modern laptop.
  • Encoder feel is firm and detented. Whether that is a strength depends entirely on whether you are riding a filter or setting a level.

Who it is for

Works well for

  • Riding several parameters at once during a performance
  • Sets where the same sixteen controls repeat across projects
  • Anyone who wants knobs without a full control surface on the desk

Wrong tool for

  • Clip launching and transport — it does neither
  • Studios that want one device to cover everything
  • Workflows that need on-device labelling; there is no screen

The archive connection

This device is why one of the historical resources on this domain exists. In 2014 the previous operator published a Max for Live device for switching its banks from inside Live; the mapping is still maintained by DJ TechTools. The background is documented on the Bang My Twister archive page.
