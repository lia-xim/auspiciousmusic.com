How to Build a Sampled Instrument in Ableton Live

Recording a viola across three dynamic layers, trimming and looping the sustains, and mapping the result into Sampler — including the two points where round-robin stopped behaving and what fixed it.

One recording session plus roughly three hours of editing

Decide the instrument before you record

The most expensive mistake in sampling is recording first. Every decision downstream — how many velocity layers, how far apart the sampled pitches sit, whether the release tail is captured separately — changes what you need to play into the microphone, and none of it can be added afterwards without going back to the room.

For this build the target was narrow on purpose: a sustained viola patch that holds up under a slow legato line, not a full articulation set. That single constraint removed spiccato, pizzicato and the entire question of transition samples, and brought the session down from two days to one afternoon.

Recording the layers

Three dynamic layers is the smallest number that still reads as an instrument rather than a sample. Two layers produce an audible step at the crossover; four rarely earns its editing time for a sustain-only patch.

  1. Record one full chromatic pass per dynamic, rather than all dynamics per note. Players hold a dynamic far more consistently across pitches than across repeats.
  2. Sample every minor third and let Sampler stretch the two semitones between. Beyond a minor third the formant shift starts to sound like a detuned copy.
  3. Leave four seconds of tail running after each note. The release is the part you cannot synthesise.
  4. Record a slate before each pass. Three hours later it is the only reliable way to tell layer two from layer three.

Trimming and looping

A sustain loop that breathes is worth more than a loop that is technically seamless. Zero-crossing alignment removes the click; it does not remove the sense that the same second of bow is repeating. Crossfading a longer window across a slightly irregular section is what makes it stop sounding looped.

The 40 ms crossfade is not a default worth keeping. On the lowest layer it smeared the bow change badly enough to hear. Layer one ended up at 90 ms while layers two and three stayed at 40 — Sampler allows the value per zone, and there is no reason to keep it uniform.

Mapping into Sampler

Sampler reads root keys from filenames if the naming is consistent, which is the entire argument for deciding the naming scheme before the session rather than during the import. Files named vla_C3_v2_t1.wav dropped into the zone editor in one pass; the earlier take, named by date, took twenty minutes to sort by hand.

Where round-robin broke

Two problems, both worth stating because neither is documented clearly anywhere we could find.

The first: with round-robin selection set to random, repeated notes occasionally drew the same take twice, which defeats the purpose. Cycling through takes in order sounds less clever and behaves better — the ear notices a repeat far more readily than it notices a pattern.

The second: the round-robin index resets when the device is reloaded, so the first note of every session came from the same take. Audible only when the patch opens a piece. Recording the takes at marginally different bow positions made the reset inaudible without touching the mapping at all.

Making it portable

Sampler references the sample files by path. Without Collect All and Save the instrument works perfectly on the machine it was built on and nowhere else — the failure this publication's DAW project guidelines exist to prevent.
